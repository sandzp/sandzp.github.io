Research talk · Biomedical NLP

Biomedical knowledge discovery from unstructured text using contextual word embeddings

Can BERT, a model that gives every word a different vector in every sentence, be distilled into one stable vector per word that captures scientific meaning better than Word2Vec?

Sandip S. Panesar School of Information, UC Berkeley Preprint · Research Square · Nov 2022 doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-2286334/v1
Welcome. This talk walks through one study end to end: why we would want to mine literature with word vectors, what embeddings are, how the transformer changes them, what I did, what came out, and where it could be used. 01 · Rationale

Most biomedical knowledge is locked inside prose

Journals, books and abstracts describe concepts, objects and events. The knowledge lives in the relationships between them, and those relationships are never written down as a table.

  • Supervised extraction does not scale. Relation-extraction models need large, manually labelled datasets for every new relation type.
  • The literature outpaces reading. CORD-19 alone held more than 500,000 COVID-19 articles and abstracts within months of the pandemic starting.
  • Unsupervised methods need no labels. If a model can learn what words mean from raw text, the geometry of its vectors becomes a searchable map of the field.
Set the problem. Knowledge in papers is relational: drug X affects receptor Y in tissue Z. Supervised NLP can extract this but needs labels for every relation, which is expensive and brittle. The appeal of unsupervised embeddings is that they learn from raw text only, so they scale with the literature.
01 · Rationale / precedent

Word vectors have already predicted discoveries before experiments confirmed them

Tshitoyan et al., Nature 2019 · materials science

Thermoelectric materials

200-d Word2Vec skip-gram vectors were trained on materials-science abstracts. Material names ranked by cosine similarity to thermoelectric surfaced novel thermoelectric compounds that were later verified.

Some of those materials had never appeared in the same document as the word. The link ran indirectly through neighbours such as chalcogenide and band gap.

Venkatakrishnan et al., eLife 2020 · biomedicine

ACE2 tissue reservoirs

The same technique applied to biomedical documents proposed new tissue reservoirs of ACE2, the receptor SARS-CoV-2 uses to enter host cells.

Predictions were validated with a molecular inference platform, which closed the loop from text geometry to biology.

This is the motivating precedent. The key idea is latent knowledge: a relation implied by the text as a whole but not stated in any single sentence. Both groups used static embeddings, and both explicitly speculated that context-aware models like BERT might do better. That speculation is what this project tests.
01 · Rationale / the gap

Both papers suggested BERT might do better. Nobody had shown how to use it for this.

BERT wins downstream tasks

Named entity recognition, QA, classification. It is unknown whether that advantage carries over to its internal word representations.

BERT has no single word vector

Each occurrence of a word gets a different vector depending on its sentence. Discovery by cosine ranking needs one vector per concept.

BERT has a fixed vocabulary

Roughly 30,000 tokens. Unseen biomedical terms are split into sub-word pieces that must be recombined.

Hypothesis. Aggregating BERT's contextual vectors over many sentences yields static-equivalent embeddings that capture biomedical meaning better than Word2Vec or GloVe, at lower cost than training on a whole corpus.
Three obstacles stand between BERT and the Tshitoyan workflow. The hypothesis builds on Bommasani et al. (ACL 2020), who distilled BERT into static vectors and beat static models on general-domain benchmarks like SimLex-999. The question here is whether that holds in biomedicine, and with which BERT variant, layer and settings.
02 · Word embeddings

A word embedding is a coordinate in meaning-space

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” Words used in similar contexts end up near each other.

  • Each word maps to a dense vector, typically 200 to 768 numbers.
  • Similarity is the angle between vectors: cos θ = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖), from −1 to 1.
  • Discovery means ranking every vocabulary word by its cosine to a query word and reading the top of the list.
Introduce the distributional hypothesis (Firth). Emphasise cosine similarity, because every result later in the talk is a cosine. The Gautret detail from the paper's Figure 1 is a nice hook: the vectors pick up who wrote about what, not only pharmacology.
02 · Word embeddings / static models

Static models learn one vector per word from co-occurrence

Mikolov et al., 2013

Word2Vec skip-gram

A shallow network predicts the words in a window around each target word. The hidden-layer weights become the embedding. Negative sampling keeps training cheap.

Pennington et al., 2014

GloVe

Factorises a global word–word co-occurrence matrix so that vector dot products approximate log co-occurrence counts.

Zhang et al., 2019

BioWordVec

FastText-style sub-word vectors trained on more than 27 million PubMed articles and combined with the MeSH controlled vocabulary.

  • Strength: one fixed vector per word, which makes ranking and clustering straightforward.
  • Cost: the model must be trained on the entire corpus, and its vocabulary is only what that corpus contains.
  • Blind spot: every sense of a word is blended into a single point.
These are the three baseline families. Note that Word2Vec and GloVe were trained from scratch on CORD-19 for this study; BioWordVec was used pre-trained. The cost point matters for the conclusion: static approaches need a full training run on every new corpus.
02 · Word embeddings / static vs. contextual

Same word, different meaning: static models cannot tell

Contextual vectors are richer, but discovery-by-ranking needs a single point per concept. This study’s method averages many contextual vectors back into one: a static-equivalent embedding.

This is the central conceptual slide. Polysemy is everywhere in medicine: culture, positive, discharge, resistance. A static vector is a frequency-weighted blur of senses. A contextual vector is sharp but tied to one sentence. The averaging trick (Bommasani 2020) is meant to keep the quality of contextual vectors while recovering a single, rankable point.
03 · Transformer architecture

BERT is a stack of 12 transformer encoder layers

  • Encoder only, bidirectional. Every token sees every other token in the sequence, left and right.
  • Pre-trained by masked language modelling. 15% of tokens are hidden and the model learns to predict them from context, plus a next-sentence objective.
  • Each layer rewrites every token’s vector using information gathered from the rest of the sentence.
  • Layers specialise. Lower layers tend to hold surface and lexical information, higher layers more contextual and task-shaped information. Which layer works best for word meaning is an empirical question, and it matters in the results.
Walk bottom to top. Tokens become vectors at layer 0, then 12 identical blocks of attention plus feed-forward refine them. The key practical point for this study: you can read a token's vector out of any of the 13 layers, and they behave differently. Plant that seed now because the results depend on it.
03 · Transformer architecture / self-attention

Self-attention lets each word pull in meaning from its neighbours

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ ) · V
  • Each token emits a query (what am I looking for), a key (what do I offer) and a value (what I pass on).
  • Query–key dot products decide how much each token borrows from every other token.
  • BERT-base runs 12 attention heads in parallel per layer, each free to track a different relation.

Why this matters for the study

Cost is quadratic in sequence length. A sequence of n tokens builds an n × n attention matrix, so short inputs are much cheaper.

Hard limit of 512 tokens. Long sentences from full-text articles can exceed it and must be discarded or split.

Context dilution. In a long sentence the word of interest attends to many loosely related tokens. The pilot study tested whether this hurts.

Keep the math light: the formula is there for the technical audience. The practical consequences are what matter: quadratic cost, the 512 cap, and the idea that short, focused contexts may give cleaner word vectors. That motivates the short-sequence design choice in the methodology.
03 · Transformer architecture / vocabulary & variants

A fixed vocabulary means biomedical terms get split into pieces

Illustrative sub-word split · the exact pieces depend on each model’s vocabulary
fever → fever   one native token, one vector
remdesivir → rem##des##ivi##r   k pieces, k vectors that must be pooled

● BioBERT v1.1

Original BERT weights and original general-English vocabulary, further pre-trained on PubMed abstracts and PubMed Central full text. WordPiece tokenizer.

● SciBERT (scivocab, uncased)

Trained on about 1.14 million Semantic Scholar papers, roughly 82% biomedical and 18% computer science, with its own scientific vocabulary built from that corpus.

Both used off the shelf from HuggingFace, with no further pre-training or fine-tuning, based on the pilot study.

Sub-word decomposition is the third obstacle from the rationale. BioBERT kept the general vocabulary, so many biomedical words are split; SciBERT built a scientific vocabulary, so more terms are native. That difference reappears in the results. The remdesivir split is illustrative, not an actual tokenizer output.
04 · Methodology / data

Corpus: CORD-19, the COVID-19 Open Research Dataset

500k+scholarly articles and abstracts
200k+full-text articles and preprints
1aggregated text file after removing metadata

Pre-processing for all models

  • Every number replaced with a special token.
  • Selective lowercasing, so abbreviations such as ACE2 survive.

Extra step for Word2Vec and GloVe

  • Common terms (stop words) and punctuation removed, as is standard for static training.
  • BERT keeps natural text, because its context depends on function words too.
CORD-19 was released by the Allen Institute and partners in 2020. It is big, domain-specific and messy, which makes it a realistic test bed for literature mining. Same corpus for everything except BioWordVec, which was pre-trained elsewhere.
04 · Methodology / distilling BERT

From many contextual vectors to one static-equivalent vector

w_c = mean( w_c¹ … w_cᵏ ) sub-word pooling within one context c w = mean( w_c1 … w_cn ) aggregation over n contexts (or all available if fewer)

Mean pooling at both steps follows Bommasani et al. (2020), who found it beat max, min and last-token pooling. Unlike Bommasani, a word with too few contexts uses all of them rather than falling back to the word in isolation, which BERT represents poorly.

This is the core method. Two averages: across sub-word pieces within a sentence, then across sentences. The result is a 768-d vector per word, per layer, per model, per n. That is a grid of configurations which the results section sweeps through. Point out the deviation from Bommasani: no isolated-word fallback.
04 · Methodology / design choices & baselines

A pilot settled the input format; four static models set the bar

Pilot study

Short sequences beat long ones

Long: corpus text split on full stops, i.e. whole sentences.

Short: sentences further split on commas into phrases.

Long sequences benchmarked worse and often exceeded BERT’s 512-token limit, so the main study used short sequences only. The pilot also found no benefit from extra pre-training.

04 · Methodology / evaluation

How do you score “captures meaning” without running a lab experiment?

Validating each predicted relationship needs domain experiments. Intrinsic benchmarks are the surrogate: vectors that agree with expert similarity judgements should encode reality more faithfully, so their predictions deserve more confidence.

Bio-SimVerb

988 biomedical verb pairs rated by domain experts.

Bio-SimLex

1,000 biomedical noun pairs, drawn from 14 ontologies and 14,000+ journals across 120+ areas.

Score

Cosine similarity of each pair, then Spearman’s ρ against the expert ratings. Higher ρ means better agreement.

Chosen over older sets such as MayoSRS and UMNSRS, which test only nouns and do not separate similarity from relatedness (Chiu et al., 2018).

Be upfront that this is a surrogate. Tshitoyan and Venkatakrishnan validated with physics and biology; that is not feasible for a methods comparison across many configurations. The logic: if embeddings rank word pairs the way experts do, their nearest-neighbour lists are more trustworthy. Spearman because only the ordering matters.

This is the efficiency result. Going from 500 to 5,000 contexts changes rho in the third or fourth decimal place. So you only need to sample a few hundred sentences per word and run inference, instead of training a model on the whole corpus. That is the practical payoff for discovery pipelines on new or fast-moving corpora.
05 · Results / which layer

The best layer depends on the model and the part of speech

Verbs peak late

Both models climb towards later layers. BioBERT verb vectors beat every static model from layer 6 onwards.

Nouns peak early

Noun performance is highest in earlier layers, especially for SciBERT (layer 3).

Curves differ in shape

BioBERT and SciBERT trace different layer-wise curves, so a layer choice does not transfer between models.

A practical warning for anyone extracting BERT vectors: there is no universal best layer, and the last layer is not automatically right. Verbs seem to need more contextual processing, nouns less. The paper's Figure 3 shows the full curves; this slide shows where they peak.

06 · Conclusions

What the study shows

  1. Contextual models can be distilled for discovery. Static-equivalent BERT embeddings beat Word2Vec, GloVe and BioWordVec, on verbs (BioBERT) and nouns (SciBERT).
  2. Model choice must follow the target words. No single variant won both benchmarks. Pre-training corpus and vocabulary both appear to matter.
  3. It is cheap. 500 sampled sentences per word match 5,000. No corpus-wide training run is needed, only inference on a sample.
  4. Short contexts work better than whole sentences, and are cheaper under quadratic attention.
  5. Layer and tokenization are first-class settings. Verbs peak late, nouns early, and native versus split words behave very differently.
Summarise in plain terms. The hypothesis from Tshitoyan and Venkatakrishnan holds, with a caveat: you have to pick the BERT variant, layer and handling of sub-words for the kind of word you're mining.
06 · Conclusions / proposed framework

A recipe for BERT-based knowledge discovery

  1. Build the vocabulary of words to mine, with a custom function or any open-source NLP toolkit.
  2. Sample n sentences per word at random, as few as 500. Prefer short sequences, around 26 words on average.
  3. Tokenize and encode with a domain BERT chosen for the word type: BioBERT for verbs, SciBERT for nouns, and consider vocabulary coverage.
  4. Extract one layer chosen for the word type, and isolate the 1×768 vector for the target word, mean-pooling sub-words.
  5. Average the n vectors into one static-equivalent embedding per word.
  6. Mine it like Word2Vec: cosine ranking against a query concept, nearest-neighbour lists, clustering.
This is the actionable takeaway from the paper. Each step is embarrassingly parallel. Steps 3 and 4 are where the results feed back in. Step 6 is unchanged from the static workflow, so existing discovery tooling still works.
06 · Conclusions / limitations & open questions

What still needs work

  • Coarse sub-word split. “At least one word has k > 1 pieces” lumps together 2-piece and 6-piece words. The effect of k needs quantifying.
  • Pooling choice. Mean pooling was best in general-domain tests, but Ács et al. (2021) found the best sub-word pooling depends on the task.
  • Benchmarks are a surrogate. Agreement with expert similarity ratings is not the same as a validated discovery.
  • Why the crossover? BioBERT keeps the general BERT vocabulary; SciBERT has a scientific one. Sub-word quality may also need more pre-training steps.
  • General-domain pairs. The benchmarks include general-English pairs, which may favour SciBERT’s mixed CS/biomedical pre-training.
  • Layer behaviour differs for native and split words in both models and is not yet explained.
Be candid here; it makes the talk stronger. The most important limitation is the surrogate evaluation. The obvious next study is to rerun a Tshitoyan-style prediction with these embeddings and validate the predictions in the domain.
06 · Conclusions / potential uses

Where static-equivalent BERT embeddings could be put to work

Drug repurposing leads

Rank compound names by similarity to a target, pathway or disease term, as in the hydroxychloroquine neighbourhood, to shortlist candidates for review.

Literature-based discovery

Surface indirect links between concepts that never co-occur, the way thermoelectric materials and ACE2 reservoirs were found.

Rapid response to new corpora

Because only inference on a sample is needed, an emerging outbreak literature can be mined in days without training a new model.

Ontology and terminology work

Suggest synonyms, cluster related terms and propose candidate mappings between free-text mentions and ontology concepts.

Exploring a field’s structure

Cluster and map concepts, for example the psychiatric terms such as anxiety and depression that sit near “coronavirus” and “symptoms” in CORD-19.

Features for downstream models

Use the vectors as drop-in replacements for Word2Vec features in existing classifiers and retrieval systems.

Close on applications. The common thread: anywhere a Word2Vec vector was used, a distilled BERT vector can be swapped in, and it can be produced for a new corpus quickly. The psychiatric example comes from the paper's Figure 2. Mention that newer work has extended static-equivalent methods (Gupta and Jaggi 2021, Phrase-BERT, hybrid static/contextual embeddings).
